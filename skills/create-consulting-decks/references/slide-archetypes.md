# Slide Archetypes

Use this reference to choose slide roles and layouts. Each slide should have one job and one governing message.

## Executive Summary

Purpose: Give leaders the answer and decision ask upfront.

Use when: The audience is senior, time is short, or the deck recommends action.

Layout: 3-5 key messages, each with evidence or implication. Avoid dense paragraphs.

## Issue Tree

Purpose: Decompose a problem into testable parts.

Use when: The work needs a clear analysis frame or when aligning on scope.

Layout: Left-to-right tree or top-down branching. Keep branch names parallel.

## Market Map

Purpose: Show where value pools, customer segments, or competitors sit.

Use when: Comparing segments, attractiveness, or positioning.

Layout: 2x2 matrix, segment table, value-chain map, or competitor landscape. Label axes with business meaning, not generic "high/low" alone.

## Options Comparison

Purpose: Compare strategic choices and make tradeoffs visible.

Use when: There are 2-4 viable alternatives.

Layout: Criteria rows and option columns. Use a final recommendation column or verdict row.

## Recommendation

Purpose: State what to do and why.

Use when: The deck has enough evidence to recommend a path.

Layout: Headline recommendation, 3 reasons, required decision, and near-term action.

## Financial Impact

Purpose: Quantify value, cost, or sensitivity.

Use when: Numbers affect the decision.

Layout: Waterfall, bridge, sensitivity table, unit economics, or metric cards. Keep charts editable.

## Roadmap

Purpose: Translate recommendation into phased execution.

Use when: The audience must understand timing, ownership, and sequencing.

Layout: 3-5 phases across time, with deliverables and decision gates.

## Risk And Mitigation

Purpose: Build credibility by showing downside awareness.

Use when: Recommendation carries execution, market, financial, regulatory, or people risk.

Layout: Risk, likelihood/impact if useful, mitigation, owner, trigger.

## Appendix

Purpose: Hold backup analysis without interrupting the main argument.

Use when: Details are useful but not necessary for the main decision.

Layout: Dense but organized. Keep source notes clear.
